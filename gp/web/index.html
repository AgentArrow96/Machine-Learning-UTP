02 · What we work with

One storm, 2,343 aerial photos.

FloodNet: drone photos taken after Hurricane Harvey, each with a hand-painted label for every pixel. We use the official split.

05001,0001,5002,0002,343
1,445 train450 validate448 test

The models learn from the training photos, pick their best version on validation, and are scored once on the test photos they never saw.

From ten labels to three

The data has ten kinds of label. We only ask two questions.

  1. 0Background
  2. 1Building flooded
  3. 2Building dry
  4. 3Road flooded
  5. 4Road dry
  6. 5Water
  7. 6Tree
  8. 7Vehicle
  9. 8Pool
  10. 9Grass
  1. Flooded building
  2. Flooded road
  3. Other

A needle in a haystack

Only 3.5% of the test pixels are flooded buildings or roads.

So a model that never finds a single flood still scores 96.5% accuracy. Accuracy hides the very thing we care about. That is why we score each model by IoU: how much its flooded area overlaps the real one.

03 · How we did it

Same photos, two ways of looking.

First we cleaned the data the same way for both, then built a traditional model and a deep-learning model.

  1. Auditevery photo matched to its label; no missing or broken files
  2. Fix orientation57 photos were stored rotated
  3. Resize to 256 pxkeeping the shape; the padding is never scored
  4. Ten labels to threebuilding, road, other
  5. 25% and 100%of the training labels, to test what more labels buy
Traditional machine learning

SLIC + Random Forest

Each patch is judged on its own, side by side on one flat plane.

Groups the photo into about 250 patches of similar pixels, describes each patch with 22 hand-picked numbers for colour and texture, then lets 150 decision trees vote on its label.

Features: chosen by us

Deep learning

U-Net

Encoder shrinks the photo to 8 px; the decoder grows it back, with skip connections carrying detail across.

A neural network that starts from a ResNet-18 already trained on everyday photos, then learns its own features from every pixel and paints a label for each one.

Features: learned from the data

A fair fight

Both models see the same photos, the same 25% subset and the same test set. Both give the rare flood classes the same extra weight, so neither wins just by handling the imbalance differently. Each setup is trained three times (seeds 42, 43, 44) so one lucky run cannot decide the result.

+18.1 pts

More labels help roads most

Four times the labels lift the U-Net's flooded-road IoU from 40.3% to 58.4%, but buildings only by +3.9 points. Roads next to open water are the hard part.

5.6 ms

Fast where it counts

The U-Net labels a photo in about 5.6 ms on a laptop GPU, nine times faster than the forest's 52.3 ms.

water ≠ road

The forest confuses look-alikes

Judged by colour and texture alone, open floodwater looks like a flooded road. Most of the forest's false alarms land there.

What we do not claim

We measured pixels, not lives saved, damage avoided or response time; those need a field study with responders. A wrong mask has a cost too: a false alarm can send a team to a dry road, and a missed road could send one into water. That is why a person stays in the loop. The photos come from one suburb in Texas, so the results should not be assumed to hold elsewhere, including Malaysian cities, without local data.

06 · Honest limits and conclusion

Knowing the score is only
the beginning.

Why do the limits matter?

  • One flood, one place. Every photo is from Hurricane Harvey.
  • 357 of 448 test photos share ground with a training photo, so the test mostly measures areas the models have already seen, not a new flood.
  • Pixels, not depth. The maps say nothing about water depth, area or whether a road can be driven.
  • A research prototype, not a working disaster-response service, with no hyperparameter search.

1Develop

Both pipelines were built end to end and produce a flood mask for every test photo.

2Evaluate

The U-Net clearly wins: 63.3% vs 31.8% at all labels and 52.3% vs 27.6% at a quarter. It trains slower (about 3.2 min) but predicts nine times faster.

3Interpret

Accuracy cannot guide this choice. Errors centre on look-alikes, and extra labels are worth more for roads than for buildings.
